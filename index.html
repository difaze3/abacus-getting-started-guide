1The mental model

Five ideas explain almost everything the agent does. Read these once and the rest of the guide becomes obvious.

Idea 1

It's a conversation, not a form

You describe an outcome in plain language. It plans, uses tools, and reports back. No syntax to learn, no configuration screens to hunt through.

In practice: there is no settings maze to memorise. If you can describe the job to a colleague, you can describe it here.

Idea 2

Tools are how it acts

Reading and writing files, running commands, searching the web, opening a browser, sending email. If it lacks a tool for something, it will tell you rather than pretend.

In practice: when something fails, ask "which tool did you try?" — the answer tells you instantly whether the fix is a connector, a file path, or a simpler request.

Idea 3

Connectors link your accounts

Gmail, Google Calendar, Drive, Slack, Notion, Jira and many more. Connecting one lets the agent work inside that service as you.

In practice: connect on demand, not in advance. The moment a real task needs your calendar is the moment to connect it.

Idea 4

Routines run without you

A saved prompt on a schedule or a webhook. This is what turns a one-off task into something that happens every morning on its own.

In practice: anything you'd put in a calendar reminder is a routine candidate. The bar is "I do this weekly and it's boring".

Idea 5

Outputs are files you can keep

Reports, PDFs, slide decks, spreadsheets, mockups and small web apps. They land in your workspace and stay there.

In practice: always check that the file was handed over, not just created. Ask "show me the file" if the preview stays empty.

Idea 6

Credits meter the work

Every action spends a little. Big jobs cost more than small ones. Watching your balance is how you avoid surprises.

In practice: a plan first is cheap; a wrong run is expensive. That single habit is most of credit discipline.

The single most useful habit

Ask for an outcome, not a procedure. "Summarise what changed in this folder since Monday" beats "list the files, then read them, then...". The agent chooses the steps; you own the goal.

2First-time setup

Fifteen minutes of groundwork that pays off in every session afterwards.

  1. Know where your workspace is

    Everything the agent creates lives in a working folder on your machine. When it says it saved a file, that's where to look. The chat shows a clickable link whenever it produces something.

  2. Connect the accounts you actually use

    Start with one or two, not ten. Gmail and Google Calendar cover a huge amount of daily work. You can connect more later without any penalty — see section 5.

  3. Check your credit balance

    Ask the agent directly: "How many credits do I have left this month?" It reads the live number rather than guessing. Knowing your starting point makes later usage meaningful.

  4. Decide your first real use case

    Pick something you genuinely do every week and dislike. A status report. Sorting an inbox. Turning meeting notes into action items. A real task teaches you more than a demo.

  5. Run one throwaway test

    Ask it to write a text file with three bullet points about your week. Confirm you can open the result. That closes the loop on the whole file-and-tool cycle in under a minute.

Worth knowing early

The agent can read and write files and run commands on this machine. Treat it like a capable new colleague with access to your desk: fine for most things, but don't hand it anything you wouldn't hand a stranger, and don't paste secrets into a prompt unless you're comfortable with them being used.

3Anatomy of a good request

Most disappointing results come from a request missing one of five ingredients. Include all five and quality jumps immediately.

IngredientWhat it answersExample
Goal What finished looks like "A one-page summary I can send to my manager"
Sources Where the material is "From the last 30 days of email in this thread"
Constraints Limits and rules "Under 400 words, no jargon, bullet points"
Format The shape of the output "A PDF" / "a spreadsheet" / "a slide deck"
Audience Who reads it "For a non-technical executive"

Before and after

Weak

prompt.txt
Summarise my emails.

Strong

prompt.txt
Read the email threads I've been part of over the last 14 days.
Group them by project and tell me which ones are waiting on a reply from me.
Put the result in a short PDF — one page, bullets, newest first —
that I can skim in two minutes before a planning meeting.

The strong version names a window, a grouping, a decision it must help you make, a format and a time budget. Nothing in it is technical.

Iterate instead of restarting

If the first attempt is 70% right, say what to change: "Keep the structure, drop the second section, and make the tone more direct." Refining is far cheaper than starting over, and the agent keeps its context.

Phrases that reliably improve output

  • "Ask me before you start if anything is ambiguous." — prevents a long wrong run.
  • "Show me your plan first." — cheap checkpoint before expensive work.
  • "Cite which file or message each claim comes from." — makes it verifiable.
  • "If you can't find X, say so rather than guessing." — kills invented detail.
  • "Keep this as a reusable routine." — turns a good result into automation.

4First project, step by step

A complete worked example. Adapt the subject matter to your own work; the shape stays the same.

The scenario: every Monday you spend half an hour assembling a status update from scattered notes and messages.

  1. State the outcome, not the steps

    "I need a weekly status update I can send every Monday morning. Here's last week's version so you can match the structure." Point it at a real file — matching an existing example is the fastest way to get the format you want.

  2. Give it the raw material

    Either name the sources ("the notes folder in my Drive", "the #project channel in Slack") or paste the content. If it's a connected service, it fetches it itself; if not, paste it.

  3. Let it draft, then correct

    First pass will be close but not perfect. Give two or three concrete corrections — "shorter intro", "call out the blocked items first". Two rounds is usually enough.

  4. Lock the format

    Once it's right, say: "This structure is now the template. Always use it." Future runs inherit the shape without re-explaining.

  5. Turn it into a routine

    "Every Monday at 8am, produce this update and leave it in the workspace." Now the task is off your plate permanently — see section 6.

The pattern to remember

Do it once by hand → correct it → freeze the format → schedule it. Almost every useful automation you build will follow exactly these four beats.

5Connectors

Connectors are how the agent reaches into the services you already use. Without them it works on files; with them it works on your actual accounts.

What's available

Account connectors include Gmail, Google Drive, Google Calendar, Slack, Outlook, OneDrive, Jira, Confluence, Dropbox, X, GitHub, Figma, Zoom, DocuSign and Google Cloud. There are also chat apps (WhatsApp, Telegram, Discord) and tool servers such as Notion, Canva, Stripe, PayPal, Zapier and Hugging Face.

How to connect one

  1. Just ask. Say "connect Gmail" or "I need to search my Drive".
  2. A Connect button appears in the chat. Click it.
  3. You sign in on the provider's own page — the agent never sees your password.
  4. Once connected, that service's abilities appear in the agent's toolset and it starts using them when relevant.

One Google sign-in covers Gmail, Drive and Calendar together.

Choosing what to connect

Connect thisIf you want to…
GmailTriage, search, draft and send mail; find threads and attachments.
Google CalendarCheck availability, summarise your week, prepare for meetings.
Google DriveRead and create documents, sheets and files in your own storage.
SlackRead channels, catch up on what you missed, send messages.
NotionTurn pages into structured notes, reports or databases.
GitHubWork with repos, pull requests and issues.
Least privilege, practically

Connect services one at a time, as a real task needs them. A connector you never use is access you never needed. You can disconnect any of them at any time just by asking.

6Routines & automation

The step from "useful assistant" to "runs my recurring work". A routine is a saved prompt that fires on its own.

Two ways to trigger one

TriggerUse it for
ScheduleAnything on a clock: a Monday briefing, a nightly tidy-up, a monthly report. Standard cron syntax, in your local time.
WebhookAnything event-driven: an outside system posts to a URL you've been given and the routine runs with that data.

Examples that earn their keep

  • Weekday 8am — "Summarise unread email from the last 16 hours, flag anything needing a reply today, put it in the workspace."
  • Friday 4pm — "Draft my weekly update from this week's notes and calendar."
  • Monday 7am — "List this week's meetings with one line of preparation for each."
  • Monthly — "Reconcile last month's expenses from these files and flag anything unusual."

Rules for writing a good routine prompt

  1. Make it standalone. It runs in a fresh session with no memory of your conversation. Every detail it needs must be in the prompt itself.
  2. Pin the output. Say where the result should go and what format it takes. "Leave it as a PDF in the reports folder."
  3. Make it safe to run when nothing changed. If there's no email, it should say "nothing to report" rather than inventing content.
  4. State the timezone. Routines use local time — confirm that matches your expectation.
  5. Expect a first run immediately. A new scheduled routine fires once straight away so you can see it work. That's normal.
Where routines live

Routines run on your computer, and only while it is on and the app is open — unless your plan provides always-on execution. If a routine must never miss, check your plan first.

Manage them by asking

"List my routines", "pause the Monday one", "change it to 7am", "delete that". No settings screen required.

7Skills

Skills are reusable instruction packs that teach the agent how you want a specific kind of job done. They are how you stop repeating yourself across sessions.

What a skill is

A written procedure the agent loads when the task matches. Instead of re-explaining your house style every time, you write it down once and it applies from then on.

When to make one

  • You've explained the same procedure three times.
  • You have a house style — a report format, a tone of voice, a naming convention.
  • There's a checklist your team must follow every time.
  • A review process with specific things to look for.

Two ways to get one

  1. Install a published one. Ask for a skill by what you want it to do — "I need a skill for reviewing pull requests" — and a matching one is found and installed for you.
  2. Write your own. Ask the agent to create a skill capturing the procedure you just walked through together. It writes the file; you refine the wording.
The compounding effect

Routines handle when work happens. Skills handle how it's done. Together they're what makes the agent feel like it actually knows your job rather than starting cold every morning.

8Documents, decks, designs

When you need something presentable rather than a chat reply, ask for the finished artifact directly.

Ask forYou getGood for
A documentA formatted PDFReports, memos, briefings, proposals, write-ups
A deckA designed PDF plus an editable PowerPointPitches, reviews, teaching, announcements
A designMockups and wireframes as imagesProduct screens, app layouts, UI concepts
A pageA live web page you can openDashboards, guides, shareable references

How to brief one well

  1. Say who it's for. "For a non-technical board" changes everything about the writing.
  2. Say what it must cover. List the sections or questions it has to answer. Content is your job; layout is handled for you.
  3. Say how long. "Six slides", "two pages", "four screens".
  4. Name your sources. Point at files or folders so it writes about the real material, not a generic version of it.
Don't over-specify the design

Asking for "a professional look" is enough. Prescribing fonts, colours and spacing usually makes the result worse, because the layout engine knows its own system better than a description can convey. Spend your words on content instead.

Editing what you got

Documents and decks keep an editable source next to the finished file. Ask for a change in plain language — "tighten the second page", "move the summary to the front" — and it regenerates rather than starting over. Existing PDFs can also be merged, split, rotated, watermarked and form-filled on request.

9Working with code

If you write software, this is where the agent is strongest — but the working habits matter more than the raw capability.

Habits that produce good results

  1. Point at the repository, not the problem in the abstract

    "In this repo, find why the login test is flaky" beats "how do I fix flaky tests". Real files beat hypotheticals.

  2. Ask for the plan before the change

    "Tell me what you'd change and why, before editing anything." A thirty-second review that catches an hour of wrong work.

  3. Insist on running the tests

    Any change worth making is worth verifying. "Make the change and run the test suite" — not just "make the change".

  4. Keep changes reviewable

    Small, focused changes you can read and approve. If a request would touch forty files, split it.

  5. Get a written review

    Ask for a review of a diff or pull request before merging. Correctness, security and clarity, with findings ranked by whether they actually matter.

You are still the reviewer

Confident output is not the same as correct output. Read the diff. Check the tests genuinely cover the change. Treat generated code exactly as you'd treat a pull request from someone you don't know well yet.

10Credits & plans

Credits are the unit of work. Understanding the shape of the cost keeps usage predictable.

What costs what

  • Cheap: short questions, small edits, quick lookups.
  • Moderate: reading many files, searching the web, drafting a document.
  • Expensive: long multi-step investigations, large refactors, generating decks or designs, running a full test suite.

Keeping usage sane

  • Check your balance when you start a session and after anything big.
  • Ask for a plan first on large jobs — a wrong plan costs a fraction of wrong work.
  • Iterate on an existing result rather than regenerating from scratch.
  • Be specific up front; vague requests wander, and wandering spends credits.

11Troubleshooting

The problems that come up most often, and what actually fixes them.

SymptomUsual causeFix
Output is generic and useless No sources, no audience, no format named Add all five ingredients from section 3. Point at real files.
It can't find your emails or files The relevant connector isn't connected Ask for it by name; a Connect button appears. See section 5.
A routine didn't run Computer off or app closed Check the routine is enabled, then check your plan for always-on execution.
It invented a fact or a file It guessed instead of reporting a gap Add "if you can't find X, say so rather than guessing". Ask it to cite sources.
It went off in the wrong direction The request was ambiguous Interrupt and correct. Ask for the plan first on the next attempt.
Credits drained fast Long exploratory runs, repeated regeneration Plan first, iterate instead of restarting, be specific. See section 10.
Nothing appears in the preview The file wasn't handed over Ask it to show you the file. Producing a result and presenting it are separate steps.
The universal escape hatch

When something is stuck: "Stop. Tell me in plain language what you've tried and what's blocking you." It works far more often than rephrasing the original request.
